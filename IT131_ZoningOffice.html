Episode 01

Street Names and House Numbers

A rookie's first day, a network that won't stop shouting, and the one line that changes everything.

Cold open

9:02 a.m. The Rookie's desk phone hasn't stopped ringing. Every printer, laptop, and phone at headquarters shares one network, and every one of them hears every ARP request and every DHCP Discover from all the others. The NOC has a name for it: The Sprawl.

Zoning Office — the address map

The Surveyor unrolls the company address map across the desk.

THE SURVEYOR

Every address on this map has two parts. A street name and a house number.

THE ROOKIE

192.168.10.77. Which part is which?

THE SURVEYOR

Depends on the mask. The mask is the zoning line: everything to the left of it is the street, everything to the right is the house. With a /26, the first 26 bits are street.

THE ROOKIE

So the address doesn't know where its own line is?

THE SURVEYOR

Never. An address without a mask is a house number without a street.

Teaching noteThe network portion (street name) is shared by every device on the subnet. The host portion (house number) is unique on it. The subnet mask, or prefix length, marks the line between them. The address alone never tells you where the line is.

Zoning Office — the stencil

The Surveyor slides a cardboard stencil over an address printed in binary.

THE SURVEYOR

Every host does this before it sends anything. The stencil has a hole wherever the mask has a 1. Whatever shows through the holes is the street. Everything under the cardboard reads as zero.

THE ROOKIE

So the host stencils its own address, stencils the destination, and compares?

THE SURVEYOR

With its own stencil, both times. Same street: walk the letter over yourself. Different street: drop it at the post office.

Teaching noteThat stencil is ANDing: 1 AND 1 = 1, anything else is 0. A host ANDs its own address and the destination with its own mask. If they match, the destination is local and the host ARPs for it. If not, the packet goes to the default gateway, the post office.

Zoning Office — two houses nobody lives in

THE ROOKIE

A /26 leaves 6 host bits. That's 64 house numbers, so 64 devices?

THE SURVEYOR

62. The first number on every street is the street sign: the network address. The last one is the street's loudspeaker: the broadcast address. Nobody lives in either.

THE ROOKIE

Every street? Even a tiny one?

THE SURVEYOR

Every street loses exactly two. A 4-address street keeps 2 houses. A 4,096-address street keeps 4,094.

Teaching noteUsable hosts = 2h − 2, where h is the number of host bits. Network address: host bits all 0. Broadcast address: host bits all 1.

EPISODE 02: EIGHT SWITCHES

Episode 02 · Training arc

Eight Switches

Training arc. Eight switches, one panel, and nobody goes home until it's automatic.

Cold open

The Surveyor taps a panel on the wall: eight light switches, each labeled with a wattage. "Nobody leaves the Zoning Office until they can read this panel without counting on their fingers."

Zoning Office — the switch panel

THE SURVEYOR

128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Flip some on. The octet's value is the total wattage.

THE ROOKIE

172 is… 128, 32, 8, and 4. So 10101100.

THE SURVEYOR

Work left to right, every time. Does this switch fit in what's left? Flip it on and subtract. Doesn't fit? Leave it off.

THE ROOKIE

And masks?

THE SURVEYOR

A mask only ever flips switches on from the left. So a mask octet can only be one of nine numbers. Learn those nine, and you'll never convert a mask again.

Teaching noteDecimal → binary: the subtraction method, left to right. Binary → decimal: add up the switches that are on. Mask octets fill with 1s from the left, so they can only be 0, 128, 192, 224, 240, 248, 252, 254, or 255. Each switch is worth double the one to its right, so there's exactly one way to make any number from 0 to 255.

EPISODE 03: WHERE DOES THIS ADDRESS LIVE?

Episode 03

Where Does This Address Live?

One ticket. Thirty-two bits. The Surveyor only needs one octet of them.

Cold open

A ticket from the help desk: "Laptop at 172.16.45.200/20 can't reach the printer. Are they on the same subnet?" The Rookie starts writing out 32 bits. The Surveyor takes the pencil away.

Zoning Office — the ruler

THE SURVEYOR

You never need all 32 bits. Only one octet matters: the first one, reading left to right, where the mask isn't 255. That's where the zoning line crosses.

THE ROOKIE

A /20 is 255.255.240.0. So the third octet.

THE SURVEYOR

Now the ruler. 256 minus the mask value: 256 − 240 = 16. Put a tick every 16 along that octet: 0, 16, 32, 48…

THE ROOKIE

The address has 45 there. The tick at or below 45 is 32, and the next tick is 48.

THE SURVEYOR

So the street starts at 172.16.32.0 and ends one before the next tick: 172.16.47.255. Everything to the right of the line is zeros on the street sign and 255s on the loudspeaker.

THE ROOKIE

That's it?

THE SURVEYOR

That's it. Every address, every mask. Six steps.

Teaching noteThe block size (magic number) = 256 − the mask value in the interesting octet. The network is the largest multiple of the block size that's ≤ the address's value there. The broadcast is the next network − 1. Octets to the right: 0 for the network, 255 for the broadcast. It's the same thing you do when you ask which inch a 3¾-inch measurement falls in, with "inches" that are 16 units wide.

EPISODE 04: CUTTING THE MAP

Episode 04

Cutting the Map

Six departments, one street, and a pair of scissors that only cut in halves.

Cold open

Headquarters' flat /24 is finally getting split up. The Surveyor lays 192.168.10.0/24 on the table as one long street and hands the Rookie a pair of scissors. "Six departments. Make me six streets."

Zoning Office — every cut doubles

The Rookie cuts the map in half. Two streets.

THE SURVEYOR

That cut cost you one host bit. You borrowed it to number streets instead of houses. Now cut every piece again.

THE ROOKIE

Four. Again, eight. I can't make six?

THE SURVEYOR

Not with bits. Cutting only ever gives you 2, 4, 8, 16. So you round up: take eight and keep two as spares. Three cuts, three borrowed bits.

THE ROOKIE

And every street got shorter.

THE SURVEYOR

Half as long with every cut. That's the whole trade: more streets, fewer houses on each.

Teaching noten borrowed bits give 2n subnets, with no −2. The h host bits left give 2h − 2 hosts per subnet. Where the scissors picture breaks: paper doesn't lose anything when you cut it, but every new street gives up 2 addresses, its sign and its loudspeaker.

Zoning Office — the holy steps

THE ROOKIE

Is there a checklist? I'm going to forget something.

THE SURVEYOR

Seven steps. Office tradition, so we call them the holy steps. Step five is where rookies go wrong.

THE ROOKIE

Why step five?

THE SURVEYOR

You add the borrowed bits to the mask you were given. Not the one you remember from the class tables. Start from a /20, and a /20 is what you add to.

Teaching noteThe 7 holy steps are below, with three worked examples. The third starts from a /20, which is exactly the step-five trap. There's also a printable copy: The 7 Holy Steps of Subnetting (PDF).

EPISODE 05: MOVING DAY

Episode 05

Moving Day

The branch office moves in Monday. The Overlap moved in Sunday night.

Cold open

The branch office, 192.168.50.0/24, is empty. Four departments and three router links move in on Monday. The Rookie has a plan. The plan is wrong.

Branch office — smallest first

The Rookie zones the three router links first, at .0, .4, and .8, then puts Guest Wi-Fi right after them, at .12/28.

[The Rookie's phone rings. It's the NOC.]

THE NOC

Zoning? WAN 1 and Guest Wi-Fi are fighting over the same addresses.

THE OVERLAP

Stencil .12 with a /28 mask and you get .0. Your "Guest Wi-Fi" runs from .0 to .15, right on top of all three links. Thanks for the room.

THE SURVEYOR

Every lot has to start on a line painted for its size. A /28 is 16 addresses, so it can only start at .0, .16, .32… never .12.

THE ROOKIE

So I skip ahead to .16?

THE SURVEYOR

You could, and leave a hole behind you. Or you do what movers do: couch and fridge first. Biggest lot at .0, then the next biggest right after it. Every lot lands on its own line automatically, and the leftover space stays in one piece.

Teaching noteThe VLSM rules: sort largest to smallest, start each subnet on a multiple of its own block size, and allocate contiguously, with the /30 WAN links last. ITN calls the same idea subnetting a subnet. The full allocation for this office is in the write-up below.

EPISODE 06: THE TOWER

Episode 06 · Season finale

The Tower

Season finale. The ISP hands over 65,536 floors, and the elevator counts in hex.

Cold open

The ISP's letter says 2001:db8:acad::/48. The Rookie reaches for the scissors. The Surveyor puts them back in the drawer.

Zoning Office — 65,536 floors

THE SURVEYOR

This isn't a street. It's a tower. 65,536 floors, and that's the subnet ID. Every floor has more rooms than you'll ever fill.

THE ROOKIE

So how do I size the floors?

THE SURVEYOR

You don't. Every floor is a /64: every department, every router link. You just choose a floor number.

THE ROOKIE

Floor 10 for Sales, then.

THE SURVEYOR

Check the elevator panel.

The panel counts 7, 8, 9, a, b, c, d, e, f, 10.

THE ROOKIE

…Floor 10 is sixteen.

THE SURVEYOR

Ten is floor a. Welcome to hex.

Teaching noteThe subnet ID is the 4th hextet: 16 bits, so 65,536 /64 subnets from a /48. Count it in hex, like an odometer with 16 digits per wheel: after 9 come a–f, and only then 10, which is sixteen. 2001:db8:acad:a::/64 is subnet ten.
