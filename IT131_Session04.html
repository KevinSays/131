Binary & decimal

A transistor is a switch — on or off, current or no current. Two reliable states, not ten. Binary isn't tradition, it's just what the hardware physically is. Every bit position is worth a power of 2.

Why this matters later This exact skill — reading an 8-bit octet as a sum of place values — is the entire mechanism behind subnetting in Module 11. Get fluent now; nobody subnets by memorizing tables, they do this math cold.

Module 5

Hexadecimal — compressed binary

Hex isn't a separate system to memorize — it's a shorthand. Every hex digit represents exactly one 4-bit chunk of binary (a nibble). Split a byte into two nibbles, convert each independently, done.

11000000 → 1100 0000 → C 0 → 0xC0 — no detour through decimal required.

Where hex actually shows up MAC addresses: AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF — 12 hex digits = 48 bits = 6 bytes. IPv6 addresses: each colon-separated group is 4 hex digits = 16 bits. Anywhere you see a long address written in hex, it's binary wearing a shorter costume.

Module 6

Purpose of the data link layer

Takes a packet handed down from Logistics (Layer 3) and prepares it for transmission onto whatever medium is actually underneath — copper, fiber, radio. On the way back up, it decides whether an arriving frame is intact and addressed to this device at all.

SublayerJob
LLC (Logical Link Control)Upper sublayer — talks to Layer 3 above, identifies which network-layer protocol is riding inside the frame (IPv4? IPv6?), independent of the physical medium.
MAC (Media Access Control)Lower sublayer — handles physical addressing (the MAC address lives here) and controls access to whatever medium is actually in use.
Naming collision, watch for it "MAC" the sublayer and "MAC address" are related but not interchangeable — a MAC address is assigned by the MAC sublayer, it isn't the sublayer itself. Be precise about which one a question is asking about.

Module 6

Media access control

Point-to-point links (two nodes, one wire) don't need traffic rules — nobody to contend with. Multi-access links (a shared medium, wired or wireless) absolutely do, or every device talks over every other device.

MethodHow it worksWhere it's used
CSMA/CDCarrier Sense Multiple Access, Collision Detection. Listen first; if a collision happens anyway, both sides back off and retry.Legacy shared-medium Ethernet — mostly obsolete now that switched full-duplex Ethernet removes collisions entirely, but the acronym is still exam-relevant.
CSMA/CACollision Avoidance. Can't reliably detect a collision on a wireless medium the way you can on a wire, so the strategy shifts to avoiding one in the first place — announce intent to send, then wait.Wi-Fi (802.11).
Controlled accessDeterministic, scheduled, or token-passing turn order — no collisions possible, but wasteful when the medium is mostly idle.Legacy/rare in modern networks — recognition-level only.

Module 6

Frame fields

Every Layer 2 frame — regardless of the specific protocol — carries some version of these fields. This is exactly the header (and trailer) that gets added when a packet becomes a frame on the way down the stack.

FieldPurpose
Frame start/stopMarks where the frame begins and ends on the wire.
AddressingSource and destination — Layer 2 (MAC) addresses, not Layer 3.
Type/lengthIdentifies what's riding inside — which Layer 3 protocol to hand this up to.
DataThe actual payload — the packet handed down from Layer 3.
FCS (Frame Check Sequence)Trailer field — lets the receiver verify the frame arrived intact.
The freshman trap, again Local Dispatch's address is never the final destination — it's just "who's next." A MAC address changes at every router the frame crosses (hop-to-hop). The IP address inside stays the same the entire trip (end-to-end). Switches forward frames unchanged at Layer 2 — only crossing a router changes the MAC addressing.

Binary → decimal

Read the octet

Convert 11100000 to decimal.

Show answer

224. Positions holding a 1: 128, 64, 32. 128 + 64 + 32 = 224. Every other position is 0 and contributes nothing.

Decimal → binary

Build the octet

Convert 150 to binary using the subtraction method.

Show answer

10010110. 128 fits into 150 (mark 1, remainder 22). 64 doesn't fit into 22 (mark 0). 32 doesn't fit (mark 0). 16 fits (mark 1, remainder 6). 8 doesn't fit (mark 0). 4 fits (mark 1, remainder 2). 2 fits (mark 1, remainder 0). 1 doesn't fit (mark 0). Result: 10010110.

Nibble shortcut

Skip the decimal detour

Convert 10101111 directly to hex without calculating its decimal value first.

Show answer

0xAF. Split into two nibbles: 1010 and 1111. 1010 = decimal 10 = hex A. 1111 = decimal 15 = hex F. Each nibble converts independently — that's the entire reason hex is faster than going through decimal.

CSMA/CD vs. CSMA/CA

Why can't Wi-Fi just detect collisions?

Legacy wired Ethernet uses CSMA/CD — it detects a collision after it happens and recovers. Wi-Fi uses CSMA/CA instead. Why not just detect collisions on Wi-Fi the same way?

Show answer

Answer: On a wire, a device can listen to the line while it's transmitting and notice the voltage pattern change if another signal collides with its own. A wireless radio transmitting at full power on its own antenna can't simultaneously listen for a faint colliding signal on the same frequency — it would just hear itself. Since collision detection isn't physically practical, Wi-Fi shifts the strategy to collision avoidance: announce intent to send and wait for a clear window instead.

MAC vs. IP

What changes, what doesn't

A packet travels from a laptop, through two switches, through one router, to a server on a different network. How many times does the source MAC address change along the way? How many times does the source IP address change?

Show answer

MAC address: changes once — at the router. Switches forward frames unchanged at Layer 2, so crossing two switches doesn't touch the MAC addressing at all; only crossing the router (a new hop) causes Local Dispatch to re-address the frame. IP address: never changes. It's end-to-end — the same source IP the entire trip, from laptop to server.
