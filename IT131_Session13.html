What Session 13 covers

Session 13 is the final exam. There's no new material; this page is for the week before. Start with the Practice Final, use its per-module breakdown to find your weak spots, and use the blueprint below to jump straight to the session page for each one.

Cold open · The OSI Company, closing time

The NOC walks the building one last time. The Front Desk is translating requests into HTTP and DNS. Translation & Wardrobe has the encryption on. Scheduling is keeping every conversation straight. Shipping & Receiving is numbering parcels and waiting for receipts. Logistics, with the Zoning Office down the hall, is routing between subnets that don't overlap. Local Dispatch is framing everything with the right MAC address, and the Infrastructure Crew has every cable labeled. "Seven floors," the NOC says, switching off the lights. "One request. You know every desk now."

One week out

A study plan that uses your weak spots

Re-reading everything feels productive and mostly isn't. Test, find the gaps, fix the gaps, test again.

  1. Take the Practice Final cold. No notes. Note your score and the per-module breakdown at the top.
  2. Pick your three worst modules. Any module under 70% counts, heaviest modules first.
  3. Review just those on their session pages (blueprint links above): the recap, the common-mistakes list, and the worked examples.
  4. Drill them. Use each session's flashcards and sample questions, then build a Practice Final run with only those modules checked.
  5. Subnetting every day. Ten minutes in the Subnetting drills. It's worth points in Modules 11 and 12 and it only gets faster with reps.
  6. Take the full Practice Final again two days before the exam. If you're over 80%, you're in good shape.

Read the question twice

Classic traps

The most-missed points from every session's common-mistakes list, in one place.

  • Network vs. broadcast: neither one can be assigned to a host. Usable is everything in between.
  • Borrowed bits: subnets = 2borrowed; hosts = 2remaining − 2. Don't subtract 2 from the subnet count.
  • ACK is the next byte expected, not the last one received.
  • Switches forward by MAC; routers forward by IP. A router is the edge of a broadcast domain.
  • Unknown unicast is flooded by a switch, like a broadcast, until the switch learns the MAC.
  • DHCP: Discover and Request are broadcast; Offer and Ack are unicast. That's ITN's answer. In real captures a server can broadcast them too (see Session 10), and a lease-renewal Request is unicast.
  • Hosts ARP for the gateway when the destination is on another network, never for the remote host.
  • IPv6 has no broadcast. It uses multicast.
  • enable secret beats enable password; service password-encryption is only type 7.
  • Router interfaces are shut down by default. Switch ports aren't, but a switch's VLAN 1 SVI is.
  • Ping symbols: ! reply, . timeout, U unreachable.
  • Worm vs. virus: a worm spreads itself; a virus needs a user.

On the day

Exam-day tips

Points lost to haste are the cheapest ones to win back.

  • Read the last line of the question first, so you know what it's asking before you read the scenario.
  • Watch for NOT, EXCEPT, and "choose two". Pick exactly as many as asked; one answer on a "choose two" loses points.
  • For subnetting, write the block size and the subnet boundaries down before you look at the choices.
  • For command questions, say the prompt out loud: R1(config-if)# tells you which mode the command must run in.
  • Eliminate first. Two of the four choices are usually clearly wrong.
  • Change an answer when you have a concrete reason, such as a misread word or a fact you remembered. Changes made for a reason help more often than they hurt.
