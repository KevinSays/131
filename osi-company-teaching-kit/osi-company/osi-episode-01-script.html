EPISODE 01 — SHOOTING SCRIPT

ONE REQUEST, SEVEN DESKS

A single browser request leaves the building, visits seven departments, crosses the wire, and comes back as a webpage. Read aloud, or assign one department per student.

COLD OPEN

Somewhere on this floor, the exact same process happens ten thousand times a day — and ten thousand times a day, nobody upstairs has any idea it's happening at all. Let's follow one request from the moment it opens its eyes at Front Desk to the moment it comes back as a page on screen.

ACT ONE — THE DOWN TRIP

Every department adds exactly one thing, then hands it to the next desk without looking back.

Front Desk — Layer 7 — Down

A student opens a browser tab and types a URL. That's the entire ask.

FRONT DESK

One request — GET /schedule.html — standard delivery.

[Writes the request in plain language and passes it on without sealing anything.]

Front Desk's whole job is "take the request." It doesn't encrypt it, doesn't address it, doesn't even know which building it's headed to. That's three floors down.

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 7, Application. PDU is just called Data. Real protocols: HTTP, DNS, SMTP, FTP.

Translation & Wardrobe — Layer 6 — Down

TRANSLATION & WARDROBE

Plain text, standard character set — nothing to translate today. If this were going out encrypted, I'd lock it in a case nobody downstream could open without the key. Not today though — it's traveling in the open.

This is the department everyone forgets exists — until they open a capture and can't read anything. That's not the data vanishing. That's this desk doing its job a little too well.

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 6, Presentation. Handles encryption (TLS/SSL), compression, and encoding (ASCII/Unicode, JPEG). Still just Data.

Scheduling — Layer 5 — Down

SCHEDULING

Booking the line now. I'll hold this conversation open until Front Desk on the other end says we're done talking.

[Doesn't touch the contents — just makes sure there's a live conversation for the request to travel inside of.]

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 5, Session. Establishes, maintains, and tears down the dialogue between hosts. Rarely shows up as its own visible header in a modern capture — wait for the twist in the Show and Tell.

Shipping & Receiving — Layer 4 — Down

SHIPPING & RECEIVING

Web traffic — that's a Certified Mail job. I want a signature at the other end, and I want these pieces back in order if I have to split them up.

[Stamps a segment header: source port, destination port 80, sequence number.]

If this had been a video call instead, this desk calls in the Bike Courier — no signature required, no waiting on confirmations. Different job, different clerk.

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 4, Transport. PDU becomes a Segment (TCP) or Datagram (UDP). Port numbers get added here.

Logistics — Layer 3 — Down

LOGISTICS

Source: this building. Destination: the server three networks over. Here's the route.

[Wraps the segment in a packet, stamps source and destination IP.]

Logistics doesn't know or care what's inside the box. Just where it's going and which router takes it next.

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 3, Network. PDU becomes a Packet. IP addressing and routing happen here.

Local Dispatch — Layer 2 — Down

LOCAL DISPATCH

I don't know any address that isn't on this block. All I know is which truck goes to the router next door.

[Wraps the packet in a frame, stamps the MAC address of the next hop — not the final destination.]

This is where freshmen usually get it backwards on the first try: Local Dispatch's label is never the final destination. It's just "who's next."

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 2, Data Link. PDU becomes a Frame. MAC addressing, always hop-to-hop, never end-to-end.

Infrastructure Crew — Layer 1 — Down

INFRASTRUCTURE CREW

Not reading any of this. Just turning it into volts.

[The frame becomes a stream of bits — electricity down copper, light down fiber, or radio through air.]

Seven departments, six headers stacked like a letter wearing six envelopes — and not one department read anyone else's envelope. That's the whole point of the model: everybody minds their own business.

TEACHING NOTE — Layer 1, Physical. PDU is just Bits. No addressing, no logic — just transmission.

ACT TWO — THE WIRE

For a few milliseconds, none of this looks like an office anymore. It's a signal — on a cable, through a switch, maybe across a few routers — until it lands at a server that has its own version of this exact same building.

ACT THREE — THE UP TRIP

Same seven desks, same order, running backward. Each one only opens the envelope it wrote.

Infrastructure Crew (Receiving) — Layer 1 — Up

INFRASTRUCTURE CREW

Got a signal. Turning it back into a frame. Not my problem past this point.

Local Dispatch (Receiving) — Layer 2 — Up

LOCAL DISPATCH

This frame's mine — MAC checks out. Stripping the envelope, handing up the packet.

Logistics (Receiving) — Layer 3 — Up

LOGISTICS

Destination IP is this building. Stripping the address, handing up the segment.

Shipping & Receiving (Receiving) — Layer 4 — Up

SHIPPING & RECEIVING

Port 80, sequence checks out, nothing missing. Reassembling and handing it up whole.

Scheduling (Receiving) — Layer 5 — Up

SCHEDULING

Conversation's still open on my end too. Keeping the line live.

Translation & Wardrobe (Receiving) — Layer 6 — Up

TRANSLATION & WARDROBE

Plain text, nothing to decrypt today. Handing it up exactly as written.

Front Desk (Receiving) — Layer 7 — Up

FRONT DESK

GET /schedule.html — got it. Pulling the file, sending a response.

And thirty milliseconds later, this whole thing happens again in reverse — Front Desk becomes the one replying, and back at the first building, an Infrastructure Crew is about to turn light back into a picture of a schedule.

SHOW AND TELL — LIVE CAPTURE

Same story, now on screen. Run this right after the read-through while it's still fresh.

Walkthrough

  1. Start a capture on the range, then generate one plain-HTTP request — a lab-hosted page is best, since most public sites now force HTTPS and won't show the readable version.
  2. Stop the capture, select the GET packet, and expand the packet-details pane from the bottom up: Frame → Ethernet II → Internet Protocol → TCP → Hypertext Transfer Protocol.
  3. Call out each section by department name as you expand it — this is the Up Trip playing out for real, in the exact order it was just performed.
  4. Now repeat the request against an HTTPS site and expand the same pane. This is the twist: Ethernet, IP, and TCP still show plainly, but everything from TLS up is opaque. Translation & Wardrobe closed the curtain — and the TLS handshake itself is Scheduling's session-opening ritual, hiding in plain sight where the model says Layer 5 shouldn't have its own visible line.

Debrief questions

  • Local Dispatch's MAC address was never the final destination — whose address was it, and how many times does it change on the way there?
  • What disappeared from the packet details when we switched to HTTPS, and which department is responsible for it disappearing?
  • If you only had this one capture and no context, which department's work would be hardest to prove even happened?
