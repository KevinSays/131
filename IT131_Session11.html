Module 16 · 16.1

Security threats and vulnerabilities

A threat is what could go wrong. A vulnerability is the weakness that lets it happen. An attack needs both.

Four types of threats

ThreatWhat happens
Information theftSomeone gets in and steals confidential data: customer lists, designs, research.
Data loss and manipulationData is destroyed or changed: a virus that wipes a drive, or someone editing a grade or a price.
Identity theftPersonal information is stolen to pretend to be someone else: to open accounts, file taxes, take loans.
Disruption of serviceLegitimate users can't get to the service. DoS attacks are the classic example.

Three types of vulnerabilities

TypeExamples
TechnologicalWeaknesses built into the technology itself: TCP/IP protocol weaknesses (HTTP, FTP, and Telnet send in plaintext), operating system flaws, and network equipment flaws.
ConfigurationWeaknesses we create: unsecured user accounts, system accounts with easy passwords, misconfigured internet services, unsecured default settings, and misconfigured network equipment.
Security policyNo written policy, office politics, no authentication continuity, logical access controls not applied, software and hardware changes made outside the policy, and no disaster recovery plan.
Which one is yours? You can't fix a protocol, but you can choose not to use it. Telnet's plaintext is a technological weakness. Leaving Telnet enabled on your router is a configuration weakness. Most of the device security in 16.4 is about closing configuration vulnerabilities.

Four types of physical threats

TypeExamples
HardwarePhysical damage to servers, routers, switches, cabling, and workstations.
EnvironmentalTemperature extremes (too hot or too cold) and humidity extremes (too wet or too dry).
ElectricalVoltage spikes, brownouts (insufficient supply voltage), unconditioned power (noise), and total power loss.
MaintenancePoor handling of key components (electrostatic discharge), no critical spare parts, poor cabling, and poor labeling.

A good physical plan locks wiring closets and equipment rooms, controls who has the key, and puts monitoring on the door. The fanciest firewall in the world doesn't help if anyone can walk in and plug into the console port.

Module 16 · 16.2

Network attacks

Malicious software, plus three families of attack: reconnaissance, access, and denial of service.

Malware

TypeHow it spreadsKey idea
VirusAttaches to a host program or file. Needs a human action (running the program, opening the file) to spread.Can't move on its own.
WormCopies itself across the network by exploiting vulnerabilities. No host program and no user action needed.Spreads very fast and eats bandwidth.
Trojan horseLooks like legitimate software, and the user installs it willingly.The damage is hidden inside the "gift".

Reconnaissance attacks: casing the building

Recon is information gathering: finding out what's there before attacking it. It usually comes first.

  1. Internet queries. Look up public information about the target: who owns the address block (whois), DNS records, the company website.
  2. Ping sweeps. Ping every address in a range to find out which hosts are alive.
  3. Port scans. Probe the live hosts to see which ports are open, so which services are running. Tools ITN names: Nmap, SuperScan, Angry IP Scanner, NetScanTools.
  4. Vulnerability scans. Ask the running services what version they are, and look up known weaknesses for those versions.
  5. Exploitation tools. Try to exploit what was found.

Access attacks: getting in

AttackWhat the attacker does
Password attacksGuesses or cracks passwords: brute force, dictionary attacks, or sniffing them off the wire (which is why Telnet is a problem).
Trust exploitationCompromises a host that another system trusts, then uses that trust to get into the second system.
Port redirectionUses a compromised host as a stepping stone to pass traffic through to a target that would otherwise be filtered.
Man-in-the-middle (MITM)Sits between two parties, reading and possibly changing what they send, while both think they're talking directly.

Denial of service: blocking the door

  • DoS: one source overwhelms a device or service with too much traffic, or with maliciously formatted packets it can't handle. The goal isn't to steal anything; it's to take the service away from legitimate users.
  • DDoS (distributed DoS): the same thing from many sources at once, usually a botnet of compromised "zombie" machines controlled by the attacker.
  • DoS attacks are relatively simple to run, even for an unskilled attacker, and they cost the victim time and money.

Module 16 · 16.3

Network attack mitigations

No single product stops everything. You layer defenses so that an attacker who gets past one still meets the next.

Defense-in-depth

Also called the layered approach. ITN's example network uses several security devices and services together:

Device or serviceJob
VPNAn encrypted tunnel across an untrusted network, for remote users and branch sites.
ASA firewallCisco's dedicated firewall. Allows outbound traffic and returning replies, and blocks unrequested traffic from outside.
IPSIntrusion prevention system. Watches traffic for known attack signatures and stops them in real time.
ESA / WSAEmail and web security appliances. Filter spam, phishing, and malicious websites.
AAA serverCentral place that checks who is logging in, what they may do, and records what they did.

Keep backups

Back up device configurations and IOS images as well as data. ITN's four backup considerations:

  • Frequency: regular, scheduled backups, e.g. a full backup weekly and partial backups daily.
  • Storage: keep copies offsite, and rotate the media.
  • Security: protect backups with passwords, and know who can restore them.
  • Validation: test restores, so you know the backup actually works.

Upgrade, update, and patch

Most worms and many other attacks exploit holes that already have a fix. Keeping operating systems, applications, and device software current is one of the most effective defenses there is. In a large organization, patches are managed centrally so they can be tested and then pushed to every host.

AAA: authentication, authorization, accounting

Question it answersCredit-card version
AuthenticationWho are you?Is this your card?
AuthorizationWhat are you allowed to do?How much can you spend?
AccountingWhat did you do, and when?What did you spend it on? (the statement)
Preview, not configured in ITN In ITN, AAA is a concept. Configuring it with a RADIUS or TACACS+ server comes in later courses. The device security you do configure this term is in 16.4.

Firewalls

A firewall sits between networks and controls what crosses. It allows traffic from inside out, and only lets traffic in from outside if it's a reply or explicitly permitted. ITN's four filtering methods:

MethodFilters by
Packet filteringIP or MAC address
Application filteringApplication type, using port numbers
URL filteringSpecific URLs or keywords
Stateful packet inspection (SPI)Whether incoming packets are legitimate replies to requests from inside. Unsolicited packets are blocked.

Endpoint security: an endpoint is any host: laptop, desktop, server, smartphone. Protecting them means antivirus and anti-malware, host-based firewalls and intrusion prevention, and a company policy for what can be installed. Endpoints are often the weakest link, because people use them.

Module 16 · 16.4

Device security

This is the part you configure. Routers and switches ship with almost no security, so these steps go on every device you touch.

Cisco AutoSecure

One privileged EXEC command, auto secure, starts an interactive wizard that turns off risky services and prompts you for passwords, banners, and SSH settings. It's a quick way to lock down a new device, but you should still know what each individual command does, because that's what the exam (and the next section) asks about.

R1 · AutoSecure (first lines only)
R1# auto secure --- AutoSecure Configuration --- *** AutoSecure configuration enhances the security of the router, but it will not make it absolutely resistant to all security attacks *** ...

Strong passwords

  • Use at least 8 characters, and preferably 10 or more.
  • Mix upper and lower case, numbers, symbols, and spaces.
  • Avoid repetition, common dictionary words, letter or number sequences, user names, relatives' or pets' names, and birthdays.
  • Deliberately misspell words: Smith → Smyth → 5mYth.
  • Change passwords often, and don't write them on a sticky note on the monitor.
Passphrases A sentence is longer and easier to remember than a random string: Ch@ngeTheW0rld! or IT131 makes 7 holy steps!. On Cisco devices, leading spaces in a password are ignored, but spaces after the first character count.

Additional password security

Four commands from ITN, plus the enable secret you already know. The passwords in these examples are 8 characters or longer, because once min-length 8 is on, cisco and class are rejected.

R1 · password hardening

Password configuration failed R1(config)# enable secret En@ble2026 R1(config)# service password-encryption R1(config)# line console 0 R1(config-line)# password C0ns0le!26 R1(config-line)# login R1(config-line)# exec-timeout 5 0 R1(config-line)# exit R1(config)# login block-for 120 attempts 3 within 60 R1(config)# end R1# show running-config | include secret|password service password-encryption security passwords min-length 8 enable secret 5 $1$Xq7f$eKERTZ5T9/IQc26ztSdjI/ password 7 04785B081C71404B484B53 ! type 7 = scrambled, NOT hashed. Any online decoder reverses it.
CommandWhat it does
security passwords min-length 8Rejects any new password shorter than 8 characters. Passwords set before the command are not checked.
service password-encryptionScrambles the plaintext passwords in the config (console, vty, username … password) as type 7, so they can't be read over your shoulder.
exec-timeout 5 0Logs out an idle session after 5 minutes, 0 seconds. exec-timeout 0 0 means never, which is bad practice.
login block-for 120 attempts 3 within 60After 3 failed logins within 60 seconds, refuse all logins for 120 seconds. Slows down password guessing.
Type 7 is not real protection Decode that type 7 line and you get C0ns0le!26 back. service password-encryption only defeats someone reading the screen. The real protection is secret, which is a one-way hash (type 5 on this router; newer IOS uses type 9). Use enable secret and username … secret, never enable password.

LineWhy
Hostname + domain nameThe RSA key is named hostname.domain. The router won't generate keys while it's still called Router or has no domain name.

Enrichment: SSH from a current Windows PC show ip ssh is from CCNA 2 (SRWE 1). One lab gotcha: with a 1024-bit key on this IOS version, the router only offers the old ssh-rsa host key type, and the SSH client built into current Windows refuses it with "no matching host key type found". On IOS-XE the running config shows the domain command as ip domain name (no hyphen); both forms are accepted.

Disable unused services

Every service that's running is something that can be attacked. Turn off what you don't use. IOS-XE routers ship with the web interface on (HTTP 80 and HTTPS 443):

R1 · find the open ports, close the web server
R1# show ip ports all Proto Local Address Foreign Address State PID/Name TCP 0.0.0.0:22 0.0.0.0:0 LISTEN [IOS]SSH-Process TCP 0.0.0.0:80 0.0.0.0:0 LISTEN [IOS]HTTP CORE TCP 0.0.0.0:443 0.0.0.0:0 LISTEN [IOS]HTTP CORE ... R1# configure terminal R1(config)# no ip http server R1(config)# no ip http secure-server R1(config)# end R1# show ip ports all Proto Local Address Foreign Address State PID/Name TCP 0.0.0.0:22 0.0.0.0:0 LISTEN [IOS]SSH-Process
Your output may look different The column layout of show ip ports all varies between IOS versions, so read it for the idea: each line is a port the router is listening on. Port 22 stays, because we just turned SSH on. On IOS-XE the documented equivalent is show control-plane host open-ports (beyond ITN).

Module 17 · 17.1–17.3

Designing and growing a small network

A small network is a few to a few dozen users, usually one internet connection, and one person (maybe you) who looks after all of it.

Choosing devices

FactorWhat to think about
CostThe device, plus redundancy, plus support and maintenance contracts.
Speed and types of ports/interfacesEnough ports, at the right speeds, of the right types (copper, fiber), with room to grow.
ExpandabilityFixed-configuration devices are cheaper. Modular devices take expansion cards when you need more.
Operating system features and servicesDoes the IOS version support what you need: security, QoS, VoIP, NAT, DHCP?

Planning the addressing

Plan the IP addressing before you install anything. Give each kind of device its own range in the subnet, so you can tell what something is from its address:

Device typeAddressingOur LAN-A, for example
Network devices (routers, switches, APs)Static, low or high in the range.1 – .9
Servers and peripherals (printers, cameras)Static, so their address never changes.100 – .199
End devices (PCs, phones, laptops)DHCP pool.20 – .99, .200 – .254
Hosts reachable from the internet (a public web server)Static—

The exact ranges are your choice. The rule is to pick a scheme, write it down, and stick to it. (PC-A is static at .10 for this page's examples, which is why the pool starts at .20.) (Picking subnet sizes is the Subnetting Field Manual's job.)

Redundancy and traffic management

  • Redundancy removes single points of failure: duplicate devices, duplicate links, redundant power supplies, and even duplicate services. It costs money, so a small network adds it where an outage would hurt most.
  • Traffic management: real-time traffic like voice and video can't wait in line. Routers and switches can use priority queuing, where the high-priority queue is always emptied first.

Applications and protocols

  • Network-aware applications talk to the network themselves: web browsers, email clients.
  • Application layer services handle the network for programs that aren't network-aware: file transfer, network print spooling.
  • The protocols you'll support are the ones from Module 15: DNS, DHCP, HTTP/HTTPS, SMTP, POP3, IMAP, FTP, SSH (and Telnet, which you'll turn off).
  • Voice and video need an infrastructure that supports them, end to end. VoIP converts analog phone signals to IP packets. IP telephony uses dedicated IP phones and a call-control server. Real-time apps use RTP and RTCP and depend on QoS.

Growing the network

Networks grow, so plan for it. ITN lists four things you need before you can scale:

ElementWhat it means
Network documentationPhysical and logical topology diagrams.
Device inventoryA list of every device that uses or makes up the network.
BudgetAn itemized IT budget, including the yearly equipment purchasing budget.
Traffic analysisWhich protocols, applications, and services are on the network, and how much traffic each uses.

Protocol analysis (a tool like Wireshark) shows what the traffic actually is. Capture during peak times, and on different segments, because traffic isn't the same everywhere. Employee network utilization looks at each user's OS and versions, applications (network and non-network), and CPU, drive, and RAM utilization. Over time these records add up to a baseline.

Module 17 · 17.4

Verify connectivity: ping and traceroute

Ping asks "can I reach it?" Traceroute asks "which way did it go, and where did it stop?"

Ping

Ping sends ICMP echo requests and waits for echo replies (Module 13). On a Cisco device, each probe prints one symbol:

R1 · three pings, three results
R1# ping 192.168.20.10 Type escape sequence to abort. Sending 5, 100-byte ICMP Echos to 192.168.20.10, timeout is 2 seconds: !!!!! Success rate is 100 percent (5/5), round-trip min/avg/max = 1/1/2 ms ! Same ping right after R2 reloads (empty ARP cache): R1# ping 192.168.20.10 Type escape sequence to abort. Sending 5, 100-byte ICMP Echos to 192.168.20.10, timeout is 2 seconds: .!!!! Success rate is 80 percent (4/5), round-trip min/avg/max = 1/1/1 ms ! A router on the path sends back "unreachable": R1# ping 192.168.20.10 Type escape sequence to abort. Sending 5, 100-byte ICMP Echos to 192.168.20.10, timeout is 2 seconds: U.U.U Success rate is 0 percent (0/5)
SymbolMeaning
!Echo reply received. The path works in both directions.
.Timed out waiting for a reply. The packet was lost or blocked, or there's no route back.
UA router on the path sent back an ICMP Destination Unreachable.
Why .!!!! is normal R2 had no ARP entry for PC-B yet. While it ARPs, IOS drops the packet that triggered the request instead of holding it, so the first echo times out. Once ARP finishes, the rest succeed. A . on the very first ping after a reboot is not a fault. (U.U.U alternates because routers rate-limit the unreachables they send; that detail is enrichment.)
Silence isn't always a broken network Windows Defender Firewall drops incoming pings by default, so ..... to a Windows PC can just mean the firewall is doing its job. Test against the router interfaces first. To stop a long ping on IOS, press Ctrl+Shift+6.

Extended ping

A plain ping from R1 to PC-B leaves from R1's outgoing interface, 10.0.0.1. That proves R1 can reach PC-B, but not that LAN-B can route back to LAN-A, which is what PC-A actually needs. Type ping with no address to get the extended prompts, and pick the source interface:

R1 · extended ping, sourced from LAN-A
R1# ping Protocol [ip]: Target IP address: 192.168.20.10 Repeat count [5]: Datagram size [100]: Timeout in seconds [2]: Extended commands [n]: y Ingress ping [n]: Source address or interface: g0/0/1 DSCP Value [0]: Type of service [0]: Set DF bit in IP header? [no]: Validate reply data? [no]: Data pattern [0x0000ABCD]: Loose, Strict, Record, Timestamp, Verbose[none]: Sweep range of sizes [n]: Type escape sequence to abort. Sending 5, 100-byte ICMP Echos to 192.168.20.10, timeout is 2 seconds: Packet sent with a source address of 192.168.10.1 !!!!! Success rate is 100 percent (5/5), round-trip min/avg/max = 1/1/2 ms

Press Enter to accept any [default]. You can type the interface name or its address. Prompts like Ingress ping and DSCP Value depend on the IOS version; the ones that matter are Target, Repeat count, Extended commands, and Source. The one-line form is ping 192.168.20.10 source g0/0/1.

Traceroute

Traceroute sends probes with TTL 1, 2, 3, and so on. Each router that drops a probe when its TTL runs out sends back an ICMP Time Exceeded, which reveals that hop. Windows calls it tracert; IOS and Linux call it traceroute.

PC-A · tracert to the web server
C:\Users\student> tracert www.it131.example Tracing route to www.it131.example [192.168.20.100] over a maximum of 30 hops: 1 <1 ms <1 ms <1 ms 192.168.10.1 2 <1 ms <1 ms 1 ms 10.0.0.2 3 1 ms <1 ms <1 ms 192.168.20.100 Trace complete.
What you seeWhat it means
Three times per hopThree probes are sent to each hop.
Hop 2 is 10.0.0.2R2 answers from the interface the probe arrived on, its link to R1, not its LAN-B gateway address 192.168.20.1.
Bare IP addressestracert tries to look up a name for each hop. With no reverse DNS, you get IPs, sometimes after a pause. tracert -d skips the lookups.
R1 · IOS traceroute, working and then failing
R1# traceroute 192.168.20.100 Type escape sequence to abort. Tracing the route to 192.168.20.100 VRF info: (vrf in name/id, vrf out name/id) 1 10.0.0.2 1 msec 0 msec 1 msec 2 192.168.20.100 1 msec 1 msec 1 msec ! Later, after something breaks: R1# traceroute 192.168.20.100 Type escape sequence to abort. Tracing the route to 192.168.20.100 VRF info: (vrf in name/id, vrf out name/id) 1 10.0.0.2 1 msec 0 msec 1 msec 2 * * * 3 * * * ... ! Ctrl+Shift+6 to stop early
Reading a failed trace The rule: the problem is at or beyond the last hop that answered. Here R2 answered, so the problem is between R2 and SRV1, or SRV1 itself. From R1, hop 2 is SRV1, so the likely causes are: SRV1 is off or unplugged (or its S2 port is down), SRV1 has a wrong default gateway so its replies can't get back, or a firewall is dropping the probes. A trace that dies at the far end is often a return-path problem.
Enrichment: why IOS and Windows can disagree Windows tracert sends ICMP echo requests. IOS traceroute sends UDP probes to high ports (33434 and up), and the destination ends the trace by answering Port Unreachable. A host firewall can drop one kind and not the other, so stars on the last hop alone can be normal.

Extended traceroute

Like extended ping: type traceroute with no address and answer the prompts. The useful ones are Target, Source address, and Numeric display (skip the slow name lookups).

R1 · extended traceroute
R1# traceroute Protocol [ip]: Target IP address: 192.168.20.100 Ingress traceroute [n]: Source address: 192.168.10.1 DSCP Value [0]: Numeric display [n]: y Timeout in seconds [3]: Probe count [3]: Minimum Time to Live [1]: Maximum Time to Live [30]: Port Number [33434]: Loose, Strict, Record, Timestamp, Verbose[none]: Type escape sequence to abort. Tracing the route to 192.168.20.100 VRF info: (vrf in name/id, vrf out name/id) 1 10.0.0.2 1 msec 0 msec 1 msec 2 192.168.20.100 1 msec 1 msec 1 msec

Ingress traceroute and DSCP Value only appear on newer IOS versions.

Network baseline

A baseline is a record of what "normal" looks like: ping and traceroute times, interface statistics, and utilization, taken at regular times while the network is healthy. When users say "the network is slow", you compare against the baseline to tell whether it actually is. Ping output is an easy start: save it, with the date and time, and compare later. Cisco devices can timestamp it for you.

Local Intrfce is R1's own port. Port ID is the neighbor's port. CDP runs at Layer 2, so it works even if IP isn't configured, which makes it great for drawing a map of a network you didn't build. detail adds each neighbor's IP address and IOS version.

Turn it off where it isn't needed: no cdp run for the whole device, or no cdp enable on a single interface (for example, one facing users or the internet).

Module 17 · 17.6

Troubleshooting methodology

Guessing works on easy problems. A method works on all of them.

The six steps

  1. Identify the problem. Talk to users, gather symptoms, and find out what changed. You can't fix what you haven't defined.
  2. Establish a theory of probable causes. List the likely causes, starting with the obvious.
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause. If the theory is wrong, go back to step 2 with a new one, or escalate.
  4. Establish a plan of action and implement the solution. Think about what the fix might break before you make it.
  5. Verify the solution and implement preventive measures. Check that it's fixed for the user, and stop it from coming back.
  6. Document findings, actions, and outcomes. The next person to see this problem might be you.
Escalation Some problems are beyond your knowledge or your access. Escalating is not failing: company policy should say when and to whom (a senior technician, or the vendor). Document what you tried before you hand it on.
The OSI Company version When something breaks, the NOC starts at the bottom and works up: is the cable in (Infrastructure Crew, L1)? Is the link up and the duplex right (Local Dispatch, L2)? Are the address, mask, and gateway right (Logistics, L3)? Then the port and service (Shipping, L4), then the name (Front Desk, L7). Most "the internet is down" tickets end at L1 or L3.

debug and terminal monitor

show commands are a snapshot. debug shows events as they happen. By default, debug and log messages appear only on the console. If you're connected over SSH, you see nothing until you type terminal monitor.

R1 · debug ip icmp over SSH
! Logged in to R1 over SSH from PC-A R1# terminal monitor R1# debug ip icmp ICMP packet debugging is on ! PC-A runs: ping 192.168.10.1 *Oct 7 14:30:01.512: ICMP: echo reply sent, src 192.168.10.1, dst 192.168.10.10, topology BASE, dscp 0 topoid 0 *Oct 7 14:30:02.519: ICMP: echo reply sent, src 192.168.10.1, dst 192.168.10.10, topology BASE, dscp 0 topoid 0 *Oct 7 14:30:03.527: ICMP: echo reply sent, src 192.168.10.1, dst 192.168.10.10, topology BASE, dscp 0 topoid 0 *Oct 7 14:30:04.534: ICMP: echo reply sent, src 192.168.10.1, dst 192.168.10.10, topology BASE, dscp 0 topoid 0 R1# undebug all All possible debugging has been turned off R1# terminal no monitor
debug can take a router down Debug output gets high priority on the CPU. On a busy router, a broad debug (like debug all) can overload it. Use the narrowest debug that answers your question, and turn it off as soon as you have the answer: undebug all (or no debug all). Four lines appear here because a Windows ping sends four echoes; the exact line format varies a little between IOS versions.

Module 17 · 17.7

Troubleshooting scenarios

The four problems ITN walks through, in the order you'd usually hit them.

1 · Duplex mismatch

If one end of a link runs full duplex and the other half duplex, the link stays up/up. Nothing alarms. It just gets slow and racks up errors, which is why this one is hard to spot. It usually happens when one end is hard-set and the other is left on auto: the auto end can't negotiate and falls back to half duplex. To force it in a lab on Gigabit copper, set both ends to 100 Mbps and only one to half:

S1 and R1 · duplex mismatch
! R1 G0/0/1 forced to speed 100 / duplex half ! S1 G0/1 forced to speed 100 / duplex full ! Console log on S1: *Mar 1 00:41:17.204: %CDP-4-DUPLEX_MISMATCH: duplex mismatch discovered on GigabitEthernet0/1 (not half duplex), with R1 GigabitEthernet0/0/1 (half duplex). R1# show interfaces g0/0/1 GigabitEthernet0/0/1 is up, line protocol is up Hardware is ISR4321-2x1GE, address is 70ea.1a5c.3e81 (bia 70ea.1a5c.3e81) Internet address is 192.168.10.1/24 ... Half Duplex, 100Mbps, link type is force-up, media type is RJ45 ... 0 output errors, 1832 collisions, 0 interface resets ... 0 babbles, 147 late collision, 0 deferred S1# show interfaces g0/1 GigabitEthernet0/1 is up, line protocol is up (connected) ... Full-duplex, 100Mb/s, media type is 10/100/1000BaseTX ... 231 runts, 0 giants, 0 throttles 489 input errors, 258 CRC, 0 frame, 0 overrun, 0 ignored

The half-duplex side (R1) sees collisions and late collisions. The full-duplex side (S1) sees runts and CRC errors. CDP spots the mismatch between two Cisco devices; between a switch and a PC, you find it from those counters. Fix: set both ends to auto, or hard-set both ends to the same speed and duplex.

2 · IP addressing problems

On IOS devices: a wrong address or mask on a router interface, or two devices with the same address. Check with show ip interface brief and show running-config. On end devices: the most common sign is a Windows PC that gave itself an address because DHCP didn't answer.

PC-A · switched to DHCP, and DHCP failed
! PC-A was changed to "Obtain an IP address automatically" C:\Users\student> ipconfig Windows IP Configuration Ethernet adapter Ethernet: Connection-specific DNS Suffix . : Link-local IPv6 Address . . . . . : fe80::4a1c:9e2f:7b3d:12a4%12 Autoconfiguration IPv4 Address. . : 169.254.87.211 Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.0.0 Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . :

169.254.0.0/16 is APIPA (Automatic Private IP Addressing). The label changes to "Autoconfiguration IPv4 Address", and there's no gateway. The PC can only talk to other APIPA hosts. Look for: no DHCP server on the segment, a bad cable or switch port, or a DHCP server on another subnet with no relay configured (relay is CCNA 2). After the fix, ipconfig /release then ipconfig /renew.

3 · Default gateway problems

Without a correct default gateway, a host can talk to its own subnet and nothing else. Here PC-A's gateway was typed as .254, which doesn't exist:

PC-A · wrong default gateway
C:\Users\student> ipconfig ... IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.10.10 Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0 Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.10.254 C:\Users\student> ping 192.168.10.1 Pinging 192.168.10.1 with 32 bytes of data: Reply from 192.168.10.1: bytes=32 time<1ms TTL=255 ... C:\Users\student> ping 192.168.20.10 Pinging 192.168.20.10 with 32 bytes of data: Reply from 192.168.10.10: Destination host unreachable. Reply from 192.168.10.10: Destination host unreachable. Reply from 192.168.10.10: Destination host unreachable. Reply from 192.168.10.10: Destination host unreachable. Ping statistics for 192.168.20.10: Packets: Sent = 4, Received = 4, Lost = 0 (0% loss),
What you seeWhat it means
Ping to 192.168.10.1 worksSame subnet, so no gateway is needed. The router itself is fine.
"Reply from 192.168.10.10"The reply comes from PC-A itself. It ARPed for .254, got no answer, and gave up. No router said anything.
0% lossWindows counts those self-generated replies as received. The ping failed anyway. Read the reply lines, not the summary.
TTL=255The reply from .1 came from a Cisco router (it starts at 255). Windows hosts start at 128.

The same thing happens to a Layer 2 switch: with no ip default-gateway it can be managed from its own subnet but not from anywhere else.

4 · DNS problems

The test that tells you it's DNS: ping by IP works, ping by name fails.

PC-A · pointed at the wrong DNS server
C:\Users\student> ping 192.168.20.100 Reply from 192.168.20.100: bytes=32 time=1ms TTL=126 ... C:\Users\student> ping www.it131.example Ping request could not find host www.it131.example. Please check the name and try again. C:\Users\student> nslookup www.it131.example DNS request timed out. timeout was 2 seconds. Server: UnKnown Address: 192.168.20.200 DNS request timed out. timeout was 2 seconds. ... *** Request to UnKnown timed-out

Routing works (TTL=126: a Windows host two router hops away). The name fails. nslookup shows which DNS server PC-A is using: 192.168.20.200, which doesn't exist. It should be SRV1, 192.168.20.100. Fix the DNS server setting, then ipconfig /flushdns to clear any cached bad answers.

"UnKnown" on its own is not the problem As in Session 10, Server: UnKnown also appears when a working DNS server just has no reverse record for itself. The clue here is the timeouts and the wrong address. Read the result lines, not the server name.

Watch for this

Common Module 16–17 mistakes

These come up every semester.

  • Mixing up threats and vulnerabilities. The vulnerability is the weakness; the threat is what exploits it.
  • Calling a worm a virus. A virus needs a host program and a user to spread it. A worm spreads itself.
  • Filing a ping sweep or port scan under access attacks. It's reconnaissance: nobody has gotten in yet.
  • Thinking DDoS means "a bigger DoS". The key word is distributed: many sources, usually a botnet.
  • Swapping authentication and authorization. Authentication is who you are; authorization is what you may do.
  • Thinking service password-encryption makes passwords safe. It only stops shoulder-surfing; type 7 is easily reversed. Use secret.
  • Forgetting a step of SSH: no domain name or no RSA keys means no SSH, and login local needs a local username.
  • Reading a . in IOS ping as "unreachable". It's a timeout. U is unreachable.
  • Panicking at .!!!!. The first ping usually times out while ARP resolves.
  • Concluding that the last hop that answered traceroute must be fine. The problem is at or beyond it: that router may be the one with the missing route or the down interface.
  • Running debug on a busy production router, or forgetting undebug all.
  • Expecting debug output over SSH without terminal monitor.
  • Treating a 169.254.x.x address as a valid configuration. It means DHCP failed.
  • Blaming DNS when ping by IP fails too. If the IP doesn't work, the name never will.

Threats & vulnerabilities

Sort the audit findings

An audit of a small office finds: (a) the router still has the default admin account, (b) there's no written security policy, (c) the server closet is next to a leaky water heater, (d) the switches are managed with Telnet. Classify each.

Show answer

(a) Configuration vulnerability: unsecured default settings. (b) Security policy vulnerability. (c) Environmental physical threat (humidity and water). (d) Telnet's plaintext is a technological weakness of the protocol, and choosing to leave it on is a configuration vulnerability. The fix is SSH with transport input ssh.

Malware

Which kind?

(1) A spreadsheet macro that infects other spreadsheets when someone opens them. (2) A "free PDF converter" that also installs a keylogger. (3) Code that scans the network for unpatched hosts and copies itself to each one overnight while nobody is logged in.

Show answer

(1) Virus: attached to a host file, and spreads when a user opens it. (2) Trojan horse: looks legitimate, installed willingly. (3) Worm: exploits a vulnerability and spreads with no user action. Patching (16.3) is the main defense against worms.

AAA

Which A?

(1) A help-desk tech can run show commands on R1 but not configure terminal. (2) R1 asks for a username and password at login. (3) A log shows that user admin changed the G0/0/1 address at 14:02.

Show answer

(1) Authorization: what you may do. (2) Authentication: who you are. (3) Accounting: a record of what you did.

SSH

"SSH just doesn't work"

A classmate types this on a new router, then tries to SSH in from PC-A and fails. Find three problems.

Router
Router(config)# hostname R1 R1(config)# crypto key generate rsa general-keys modulus 1024 % Please define a domain-name first. R1(config)# line vty 0 4 R1(config-line)# transport input telnet R1(config-line)# login local
Show answer

1. No ip domain-name, so the RSA keys were never generated and the SSH server never started. The router said so. 2. transport input telnet refuses SSH; it should be transport input ssh. 3. login local with no username … secret … means there's no account to log in with. Also worth adding: ip ssh version 2.

Ping symbols

Read the dots

From R1, four pings to four places give: (1) !!!!! (2) .!!!! (3) ..... (4) U.U.U. What does each tell you?

Show answer

(1) Every echo got a reply: the path works both ways. (2) The first one timed out while ARP resolved; normal. (3) Every echo timed out: lost, blocked, or no route back. Check the target is up, its gateway is right, and whether a firewall drops ping. (4) A router on the path is sending back Destination Unreachable, so it has no route or is filtering. The Us tell you where to look.

Traceroute

Where did it stop?

From PC-A, tracert 192.168.20.10 shows hop 1 192.168.10.1, then * * * Request timed out. for every hop after that. PC-A can ping its gateway. Where is the problem?

Show answer

At or beyond the last hop that answered: R1. Nothing past R1 replies, so suspect the R1–R2 link (show ip interface brief on both routers, CDP to see if they're even neighbors), R1's routing to 192.168.20.0/24 (show ip route), or R2. PC-A's own settings are probably fine, because hop 1 worked.

Scenarios

Name the problem

(a) A PC shows 169.254.3.9, mask 255.255.0.0. (b) A PC pings everything by IP, but browsers say "server not found". (c) A link is up/up but slow, and show interfaces shows late collisions. (d) A PC can ping every host on its LAN but nothing beyond.

Show answer

(a) DHCP failed: APIPA. (b) DNS. (c) Duplex mismatch; you're looking at the half-duplex side. (d) Default gateway: missing or wrong.
